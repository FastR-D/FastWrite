# FastWrite academic writing contract

This contract applies to planning, drafting, completion, revision, and review. A fluent sentence is never more important than a true claim. The author remains responsible for every claim, citation, result, disclosure, and submission decision.

## SHOULD DO

- Separate observed, computed, proved, inferred, hypothesized, and planned statements. Preserve uncertainty and scope qualifiers.
- Give each paragraph one rhetorical job: state a concrete claim or question, present the evidence or reasoning, then state its bounded implication.
- Use stable technical terms. Prefer concrete subjects and verbs; use active voice when the actor or responsibility matters.
- Quantify comparisons with the metric, baseline, dataset or population, experimental condition, and uncertainty or statistical test when available.
- Keep a claim-to-evidence trail. Place citations next to the claim they support and verify the cited work's identity and relevance.
- Preserve LaTeX commands, formulas, labels, references, citation keys, units, numbers, and established notation unless the task explicitly changes them.
- Use present tense for what the paper shows or defines and past tense for experiments actually run, unless the field's convention requires otherwise.
- Write the abstract as problem and scope, precise gap, method, principal evidence, and bounded conclusion. Only include results present in the manuscript.
- Mark missing evidence with an explicit placeholder such as `[EVIDENCE REQUIRED]`; turn intended work into a plan, never into a completed result.
- Keep ideation, evidence collection, drafting, revision, and independent review as distinguishable workflow stages. Review should diagnose before rewriting.
- Record meaningful generative-AI assistance when the target venue, publisher, institution, or research process requires disclosure.

## SHOULDN'T DO

- Do not invent or silently alter citations, authors, titles, venues, years, identifiers, datasets, methods, experiments, statistics, quotations, reviewer feedback, or venue rules.
- Do not cite a source merely because its title is related. Do not turn a search snippet, model memory, or unverified BibTeX record into evidence.
- Do not describe planned, requested, simulated, or missing work as completed. Do not use `prove` for empirical evidence that only supports or suggests a conclusion.
- Do not claim `first`, `state of the art`, `novel`, `significant`, `robust`, `general`, or `safe` without the necessary search, comparison, test, and scope.
- Do not use inflated or empty openings such as “in today's rapidly evolving landscape,” “it is worth noting,” “groundbreaking,” “revolutionary,” “pivotal,” or “delve into.”
- Do not mechanically repeat “Moreover,” “Furthermore,” or “Additionally”; force every argument into three-item lists; overuse em dashes, parentheses, rhetorical questions, or sentence fragments; or rotate synonyms for one technical concept.
- Do not leave vague pronouns such as “this” or “it” without a clear antecedent. Do not hide the actor with passive voice when responsibility matters.
- Do not add defensive caveats to every sentence or bury actual limitations. State each material limitation once, specifically, in the relevant place.
- Do not put revision-state prose such as “we added in this version” into the manuscript unless writing a rebuttal or revision letter.
- Do not optimize text to evade AI detectors or disguise provenance. Improve precision, evidence, and readability instead.

## Citation and evidence gate

Before introducing or retaining a reference, verify it against an authoritative record such as the publisher, DOI registration, proceedings site, arXiv record, or trusted scholarly index. Match title, authors, year, venue, and DOI/arXiv identifier; check retraction or correction status when relevant; and confirm that the source supports the nearby claim. If online verification is unavailable, preserve the candidate as unresolved and do not present it as verified.

## Editing boundary

For polishing requests, change clarity and organization without changing factual content. For drafting requests, use only supplied or verified evidence. For planning requests, label hypotheses, experiments, and expected outcomes as future work. For review requests, cite manuscript locations and distinguish hard violations, evidence gaps, and optional style preferences.
